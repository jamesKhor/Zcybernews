## Executive Summary

APT28, the Russian military intelligence-linked group also known as Fancy Bear, breached a naval shipbuilder, a military aircraft engine manufacturer, and a law enforcement agency in two NATO member states, according to research published by Symantec. The intrusions relied on the Hookedge backdoor — first documented in August by Recorded Future's Insikt Group — and an undocumented tool that decrypts and executes code directly in memory.

The campaign is a concrete reminder that APT28 continues to prioritize European defense and government targets, and that its tooling is evolving to include memory-resident execution. Defenders in defense, aerospace, and government sectors should treat malicious Word attachments and credential-theft activity as high-priority signals, and should hunt for Hookedge indicators.

## Technical Analysis

Symantec attributed the attacks to APT28, a group linked to Russia's GRU, and said the victims were located in Europe. The researchers did not name the two NATO countries or the specific organizations, citing victim sensitivity.

Initial access in at least one case — the naval shipbuilder — came through a malicious Microsoft Word document sent as an email attachment in July. An employee opened the document, after which the attackers explored the internal network and attempted to steal login credentials. Over the following week, the hackers repeatedly returned to a compromised computer and deployed a previously undocumented tool designed to decrypt and execute malicious code directly in the computer's memory. Symantec said the tool's ultimate purpose remains unclear.

The first confirmed infection occurred on July 24, and related malicious activity was detected as late as September 24. That timeline suggests the operation spanned at least two months, though Symantec could not establish whether the attackers maintained uninterrupted access throughout that period.

Dick O'Brien, an intelligence analyst at Symantec, told Recorded Future News that researchers could not determine whether the attackers succeeded in stealing sensitive information. "Given the nature of the targets, it's reasonable to assume that defense-related information was the objective of the attacks," O'Brien said. "In some cases, the attackers were on the network for a few weeks, which may have given them opportunities to obtain data."

Symantec assessed that the selection of targets indicates the attacks were part of a broader Russian intelligence-gathering effort focused on European government and defense organizations. O'Brien said the continued activity observed in September, following Recorded Future's initial findings in August, suggests the espionage campaign is ongoing.

The Hookedge backdoor was first documented by Recorded Future's Insikt Group in August. In this campaign, it was used to gain access to victims' systems, steal credentials, and gather information about their networks. The combination of a documented backdoor with a previously unseen in-memory execution tool suggests the group maintains a toolkit that it updates between operations.

## Tactics, Techniques & Procedures

The campaign's sequencing follows a familiar APT28 pattern: a spearphishing attachment delivers a document that, when opened, establishes a foothold; the attackers then conduct internal reconnaissance and credential theft, and deploy additional tooling to maintain access and execute code stealthily.

The use of an in-memory execution tool is notable because it reduces the forensic footprint on disk. Symantec did not provide the tool's name or hashes, so defenders cannot yet hunt for it by indicator. The Hookedge backdoor, however, has been documented by Insikt Group, and organizations should review that reporting for host- and network-based signatures.

The targeting of a naval shipbuilder, an aircraft engine manufacturer, and a law enforcement agency aligns with APT28's long-standing focus on military, government, and defense organizations in Europe and North America. Western agencies have repeatedly warned about the group's efforts to compromise organizations involved in defense and military logistics, particularly those supporting Ukraine.

## Threat Actor Context

APT28, also tracked as Fancy Bear, BlueDelta, and Forest Blizzard, has conducted cyber-espionage operations against military, government, and defense organizations in Europe and North America for more than a decade. The group gained international attention in 2016 after being linked to the hacking of the Democratic National Committee ahead of the U.S. presidential election.

In April, British security officials warned that APT28 had exploited vulnerabilities in internet routers to redirect web traffic and spy on victims. Earlier this year, researchers identified activity linked to the group targeting maritime, transportation, and diplomatic organizations in countries including Poland, Slovenia, Turkey, Greece, and the United Arab Emirates. Last year, more than 20 Western intelligence and cybersecurity agencies issued a joint advisory accusing APT28 of targeting logistics providers and technology companies involved in delivering foreign assistance to Ukraine.

The current campaign's use of Hookedge and an in-memory execution tool indicates the group continues to invest in tooling that can evade detection while maintaining long dwell times.

## Mitigations & Recommendations

Symantec did not publish specific indicators of compromise for this campaign, and the in-memory tool remains undocumented. Defenders should focus on behavioral detection: monitor for Word documents spawning unusual child processes, credential-access attempts from user workstations, and lateral movement from hosts that recently opened email attachments. Organizations that have deployed detection for the Hookedge backdoor based on Insikt Group's August reporting should verify coverage and review logs for the July–September timeframe.

Given the targeting pattern, defense contractors, aerospace manufacturers, and government agencies in Europe should prioritize hunting for the described behaviors and should treat any confirmed Hookedge infection as a likely espionage incident rather than commodity malware. Network segmentation and restrictions on macro execution from internet-originated documents remain relevant controls, though the sources did not specify the exact mechanism the Word document used to execute code.
